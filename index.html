The Complete Guide to Working with Fractions

What a fraction really means

A fraction is simply a way of writing a division that has not been carried out yet. When you write 3/4, you are saying "3 divided by 4" — three whole things shared equally among four people, or three parts out of four equal slices of a whole. The number on top, the numerator, counts how many parts you have. The number on the bottom, the denominator, tells you how many equal parts make one whole. This two-part structure is what makes fractions so powerful: they can describe exact values that decimals can only approximate. The fraction 1/3, for instance, is exact — it means one part of three — while its decimal form 0.333… goes on forever without ever quite finishing. Understanding this division-meaning is the key to every fraction skill that follows, because adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions are all just organized ways of handling that unfinished division.

Fractions show up far beyond the classroom. Cooks halve and double recipes with them, carpenters measure in sixteenths of an inch, musicians count beats in 3/4 time, and pharmacists calculate doses with them. Any tool that claims to teach fractions should therefore do more than spit out an answer: it should show the reasoning, step by step, so the skill transfers to real life. That is the philosophy behind the solver above — every result comes with a full teacher-style board of working you can follow, copy, or print.

Adding and subtracting: why denominators must match

You can only add or subtract fractions directly when they share a denominator, and the reason is wonderfully concrete. Adding 1/4 + 2/4 works because both fractions are measured in the same unit — quarters. One quarter plus two quarters is three quarters, and the denominator simply stays put: 3/4. But 1/2 + 1/3 mixes two different units — halves and thirds — the way adding 1 metre to 1 yard mixes units. Before combining them you must convert both to a common unit, and in fraction-land that common unit is a common denominator.

Any common multiple of the two denominators will do the job, but the least common denominator (LCD) keeps the numbers small and the working tidy. For 1/2 + 1/3, the denominators are 2 and 3, and the smallest number both divide into is 6. Convert: 1/2 becomes 3/6 (multiply top and bottom by 3) and 1/3 becomes 2/6 (multiply top and bottom by 2). Now the units match and you add the numerators: 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6. Subtraction works identically — the minus sign simply applies to the second numerator, so 5/6 − 1/4 becomes 10/12 − 3/12 = 7/12. One classic mistake is to add the denominators as well as the numerators (1/2 + 1/3 ≠ 2/5); remembering that the denominator is the unit, not a quantity, makes that error impossible.

Finding the LCD with prime factorization

For small denominators you can spot the LCD by listing multiples, but the reliable method for any size of number is prime factorization. Break each denominator into its prime building blocks, take the highest power of every prime that appears, and multiply those together. Consider 1/8 + 5/12. Factor the denominators: 8 = 2³ and 12 = 2² × 3. The primes involved are 2 and 3. The highest power of 2 is 2³ (from the 8), and the highest power of 3 is 3¹ (from the 12). Multiply: 2³ × 3 = 8 × 3 = 24. So the LCD is 24, and you can verify it: 24 is the smallest number divisible by both 8 and 12.

Why does this method always work? Each denominator's prime factorization describes exactly what it is "made of". To build a number divisible by both denominators, you need at least as much of each prime as the hungrier denominator demands — that is precisely the highest power of each prime. Multiplying those highest powers therefore constructs the smallest number containing both factorizations, which is the least common multiple of the denominators. There is also a handy shortcut for two denominators: LCD(a, b) = (a × b) ÷ GCD(a, b). For 8 and 12, GCD(8, 12) = 4, so (8 × 12) ÷ 4 = 96 ÷ 4 = 24. The solver above uses the prime-factor method by default because it teaches the deeper idea, and switches to the GCD shortcut only when denominators grow too large for a readable factor tree.

Simplifying to lowest terms with the GCD

A fraction is in lowest terms when its numerator and denominator share no common factor other than 1 — for example 3/4 is in lowest terms, but 6/8 is not, because both numbers are divisible by 2. Simplifying means dividing the top and bottom by their greatest common divisor (GCD), the largest number that divides both evenly. Take 48/180: the GCD of 48 and 180 is 12, so 48 ÷ 12 = 4 and 180 ÷ 12 = 15, giving 4/15. Simplified answers are the expected convention in schoolwork and make later calculations far easier, since smaller numbers are simpler to combine.

Finding the GCD by hand is fastest with the Euclidean division method: divide the larger number by the smaller, then divide the divisor by the remainder, repeating until a remainder of 0 appears — the last non-zero remainder is the GCD. For 48 and 180: 180 = 48 × 3 + 36, then 48 = 36 × 1 + 12, then 36 = 12 × 3 + 0, so the GCD is 12. This ancient algorithm, over two thousand years old, is still the quickest way to reduce a fraction, and it is exactly the working the Simplify tool above displays for every input. A fraction whose numerator is 0, such as 0/5, is already fully simplified to 0, while a zero denominator is never allowed — division by zero has no meaning, and the solver will tell you so plainly.

Mixed numbers and improper fractions

A mixed number like 2 1/3 pairs a whole number with a proper fraction, and it is how we naturally speak about quantities: "two and a third cups of flour". An improper fraction like 7/3 has a numerator at least as large as its denominator, and it is how fractions prefer to be calculated with. Converting a mixed number to an improper fraction is a two-second routine: multiply the whole number by the denominator, add the numerator, and keep the denominator. So 2 1/3 = (2 × 3 + 1)/3 = 7/3. Going the other way, divide the numerator by the denominator: the quotient is the whole part and the remainder becomes the new numerator. For 7/3: 7 ÷ 3 = 2 remainder 1, so 7/3 = 2 1/3.

Always convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before adding, subtracting, multiplying or dividing — it prevents the very common error of operating on the whole parts and fraction parts separately and forgetting to carry. Negative mixed numbers deserve care: −2 1/3 means −(2 1/3), which is −7/3, not −5/3. Keep the minus sign with the whole value and apply it to everything. The solver's toggle lets you flip any operand or answer between mixed and improper form instantly, so you can enter a problem the way it was given and read the answer the way you need it.

Multiplying and dividing fractions

Multiplication is the kindest fraction operation: no common denominator needed. Multiply the numerators together, multiply the denominators together, and simplify. So 3/4 × 8/9 = 24/36 = 2/3. A lovely efficiency trick is to cancel common factors before multiplying: in 3/4 × 8/9, the 3 and 9 share a factor of 3, and the 4 and 8 share a factor of 4, so you can reduce first and multiply small numbers instead. Multiplying by a whole number is just as easy — write the whole number over 1 first: 5 × 2/3 = 5/1 × 2/3 = 10/3 = 3 1/3.

Division looks scarier but reduces to multiplication with one extra step: flip the second fraction and multiply. This "invert and multiply" rule works because dividing by a number is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal — asking "how many 2/3s fit into 5/6?" is equivalent to scaling 5/6 by 3/2. So 5/6 ÷ 2/3 becomes 5/6 × 3/2 = 15/12 = 5/4 = 1 1/4. One hard rule: you can never divide by a fraction equal to zero, such as 0/5, because that would mean dividing by zero itself. The solver catches this case and explains it instead of producing nonsense.

Converting between fractions, decimals and percentages

Fractions, decimals and percentages are three costumes worn by the same numbers, and fluency means changing costume at will. To turn a fraction into a decimal, divide the numerator by the denominator: 3/8 = 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375. To turn that decimal into a percentage, multiply by 100 (which just shifts the decimal point two places right): 0.375 = 37.5%. Going the other direction, a percentage becomes a fraction over 100 — 37.5% = 37.5/100 = 375/1000 = 3/8 after simplifying — and a decimal becomes a fraction over a power of 10: 0.125 = 125/1000 = 1/8.

Some fractions refuse to terminate as decimals. Dividing 1 by 3 gives 0.333… with the 3 repeating forever, because no power of 10 is divisible by 3. Mathematicians write this as 0.(3), with the repeating digits in parentheses; a mixed case like 1/6 = 0.1666… is written 0.1(6). A quick way to predict termination: simplify the fraction, then look at the denominator's prime factors — if they are only 2s and 5s, the decimal terminates; any other prime means it repeats. So 7/40 terminates (40 = 2³ × 5) while 5/12 repeats (12 = 2² × 3). The converter above detects repeating cycles automatically and shows both the exact repeating form and a rounded approximation, plus the percentage equivalent.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Most fraction errors come from a handful of habits. First, adding denominators: 1/2 + 1/3 is 5/6, never 2/5 — the denominator names the unit, and units must match before adding. Second, forgetting to simplify: an answer of 4/6 is correct but unfinished; the expected form is 2/3. Third, mishandling mixed numbers in multiplication: 2 1/2 × 3 is not 6 1/2 — convert first (5/2 × 3/1 = 15/2 = 7 1/2). Fourth, sign errors with negatives: −3/4 + 1/2 = −1/4, and subtracting a negative adds: 1/2 − (−1/4) = 3/4. Fifth, dividing in the wrong order: a ÷ b flips b, never a. The surest defense is the habit this page is built around: write every step down. Estimation helps too — before calculating 7/8 + 9/10, notice both are near 1, so the answer should be near 2 (it is 71/40 = 1 31/40). If your exact answer disagrees wildly with your estimate, re-check the working rather than the arithmetic.

Practice pointers for students and parents

Mastery of fractions is built in layers. Start with equivalent fractions — the idea that 1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 — because every later skill is an application of it. Then practice simplifying with the GCD until reducing feels automatic; it halves the size of every subsequent calculation. Next, drill the LCD method on pairs like 3/8 and 5/12 until the prime-factor routine is muscle memory. Only then combine everything in mixed-operation problems. When checking homework, the printable view on this page produces a clean problem-and-steps sheet that mirrors what teachers expect to see: the LCD reasoning, the equivalent fractions, the operation, the GCD simplification, and the final mixed number. Copy the steps by hand rather than just reading them — writing the working yourself is what moves the method from the screen into long-term memory. And whenever a result looks suspicious, convert it to a decimal for a sanity check: if 2/3 + 1/4 gave you 11/12 ≈ 0.917, a quick mental check (0.667 + 0.25 = 0.917) confirms you are right.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the least common denominator (LCD) and why do I need it?

The LCD is the smallest number that all the denominators in your problem divide into evenly. You need it to add or subtract fractions, because fractions can only be combined when they share a denominator. Fracify finds it by prime-factorizing each denominator, taking the highest power of every prime, and multiplying them together.

How do I add fractions with different denominators?

Find the LCD, rewrite each fraction as an equivalent fraction with the LCD on the bottom (multiplying top and bottom by the same number), then add the numerators and keep the denominator. Finally, simplify. Example: 1/2 + 1/3 = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.

How do I simplify a fraction to lowest terms?

Find the greatest common divisor (GCD) of the numerator and denominator — the Euclidean division method is fastest — then divide both by it. For 24/36, GCD(24, 36) = 12, so 24/36 = 2/3. The Simplify tool above shows every division step.

How do I convert a fraction to a decimal or a percentage?

Divide the numerator by the denominator for the decimal (3/8 = 0.375), then multiply by 100 for the percentage (37.5%). Repeating decimals like 1/3 = 0.333… are written 0.(3). The converter detects repeating cycles automatically.

What is the difference between a mixed number and an improper fraction?

A mixed number pairs a whole number with a fraction (2 1/3); an improper fraction has a numerator at least as large as its denominator (7/3). They are the same value: 2 1/3 = (2 × 3 + 1)/3 = 7/3. Always convert mixed numbers to improper form before calculating.

Why does dividing by a fraction mean multiplying by its reciprocal?

Division is the inverse of multiplication, so dividing by a number equals multiplying by its reciprocal. 5/6 ÷ 2/3 becomes 5/6 × 3/2 = 15/12 = 5/4 = 1 1/4. The solver shows the flip step explicitly so the rule never feels like magic.
